Alan Turing was an English mathematician, computer scientist, and cryptanalyst born on June 23, 1912, in London, United Kingdom. In 1936, Turing introduced the concept of a universal computing machine, now called the Turing machine, which established the foundational theoretical model for modern general-purpose computers. During World War II, Turing worked at Bletchley Park for the Government Code and Cypher School (GC&CS), playing a pivotal role in cracking intercepted coded messages. He designed an electromechanical machine known as the Bombe, which successfully deciphered the German Enigma machine cipher on an industrial scale. In 1945, Turing joined the National Physical Laboratory where he designed the Automatic Computing Engine (ACE), one of the first stored-program computer designs. In his 1950 paper titled "Computing Machinery and Intelligence," Turing proposed an operational test for machine intelligence originally known as the Imitation Game, today widely called the Turing Test. Turing was elected a Fellow of the Royal Society in 1951. He passed away on June 7, 1954, in Wilmslow, Cheshire. In 2013, Queen Elizabeth II granted Alan Turing a posthumous royal pardon, recognizing his monumental contributions to wartime intelligence and computer science.
